Cyber Basics

What is Phishing?

Understanding the psychology and mechanisms behind modern social engineering attacks.

A Deceptive Cyber Threat

Phishing is a form of social engineering where attackers impersonate trustworthy entities (like banks, IT departments, or utilities) to trick individuals into revealing sensitive credentials, installing malware, or transferring funds.

Instead of hacking through complex security barriers directly, cybercriminals exploit the weakest link in the security chain: human trust.

Impersonation

Spoofing branding, logos, and sender details to look legitimate.

Urgency & Fear

Creating false emergencies (e.g. account suspension) to force quick action.

Credential Harvesting

Directing users to fake login pages specifically designed to steal passwords.

Attack Vectors

Common Types of Phishing

Cybercriminals deploy a wide array of methods across different communication channels. Know your enemy.

Email Phishing

Mass Campaign

Mass-scale spam emails that impersonate services (like PayPal, Netflix, or banks) designed to harvest login credentials from the general public.

High Risk

Spear Phishing

Targeted Attack

Highly customized emails directed at specific individuals. Attackers research targets (using LinkedIn, social media) to craft convincing personal storylines.

Critical Risk

Smishing (SMS)

Mobile Message

Phishing attempts conducted via SMS texts. They often claim package deliveries are blocked, or bank transactions require immediate verification links.

Moderate-High

Vishing (Voice)

Phone Call

Voice-based calls where attackers use spoofed phone numbers, pre-recorded messages, or AI voice cloning to impersonate bank representatives or tax authorities.

High Risk
Anatomy of Deception

How to Spot the Red Flags

Phishing scams are designed to look identical to official notices. Learn to spot the hidden indicators of fraud.

Red Flag Checklist

Phishers want you to act emotionally and instantly. Watch out for these 5 classic red flags in any incoming email or message:

  • 01
    Suspicious Sender Domains:

    Always inspect the domain after the "@". Spoofed addresses look like `billing@paypa1.com` instead of `paypal.com`.

  • 02
    Urgent or Threatening Language:

    Sentences like "ACT NOW OR YOUR ACCOUNT WILL BE CLOSED!" are designed to cause panic and bypass logical thinking.

  • 03
    Generic Greetings:

    "Dear Valued Customer" or "Dear User" are used because mass-campaign phishers don't know your actual name.

  • 04
    Masked or Misdirected Hyperlinks:

    The text of the link says `paypal.com`, but hovering your mouse over it shows a completely different, suspicious destination URL.

  • 05
    Unsolicited Attachments:

    Invoices, receipts, or PDF/ZIP attachments you didn't ask for. Opening them can run scripts that infect your machine with malware.

Defensive Action

Security Tips & Best Practices

Building active security habits keeps you protected even if you accidentally click a malicious link.

1. Turn on Multi-Factor (MFA)

Enable MFA on all accounts. Even if a phisher steals your login name and password, they cannot gain entry without the dynamic verification code sent to your authenticator app.

2. Check Link Addresses

Hover over hyperlinks before clicking to see the actual target URL in the bottom status bar. If the URL doesn't match the company's real domain, do not click.

3. Use Password Managers

Password managers auto-fill credentials based on the exact registered URL. If you land on a fake site (e.g., netflx.com), the manager will refuse to autofill, indicating a phishing attempt.

4. Stop & Think Under Urgency

Scammers intentionally use high pressure to trigger impulse reactions. Take a deep breath. Stop, look up the official customer service contact, and verify independently.

5. Keep Devices Updated

Install updates for your web browsers, operating system, and security software. Updates patch security vulnerabilities that attackers exploit when you visit infected sites.

6. Report Phishing Campaigns

If you spot a phishing email at work or on private accounts, report it to your IT helpdesk, or forward email spams to the company's official security inbox (e.g. abuse@bank.com).

Case Studies

Real-Life Phishing Scenarios

Compare a real corporate notification against a fraudulent copycat side-by-side.

Legitimate Bank Notice
From: service@paypal.com
Greeting: Dear John Doe (Personalized)

Your monthly account statement is ready. Log in to your account at paypal.com to view details. We will never ask you to input credit card details via direct links in this mail.

Go To PayPal.com

Safe Signs:

  • Sent from the verified official domain (`@paypal.com`).
  • Addressed to your full registered name (`Dear John Doe`).
  • Does not request high-security inputs (like passwords or full credit card digits) directly in links.
Phishing Clone
From: updates-paypal@paypa1-support-billing.com
Greeting: Dear User (Generic)

YOUR ACCOUNT HAS BEEN TEMPORARILY BLOCKED due to suspicious activity. Confirm your credentials now to reactivate and verify identity immediately.

Unlock Account Now

Malicious Signs:

  • Spelled incorrectly domain name (`paypa1-support-billing.com`).
  • Extreme threat level used to induce instant panic.
  • Generic greeting is used instead of your name.
Official Tracking Text
USPS Notification
USPS Status: Package 9400111899562721839212 is out for delivery. Monitor status at usps.com/tracking. No action needed.

Safe Signs:

  • Contains valid, trackable order numbers.
  • Link directs to the official base domain (`usps.com`).
  • No immediate fees are demanded.
Smishing Text Attack
+1 (405) 555-8912
USPS Alerts: Your shipment is on hold due to wrong address details. Pay $1.50 redirection fees within 12 hours or item returns to sender: post-office-redirection-fees.info/pay

Malicious Signs:

  • Sent from a generic mobile number rather than shortcode gateway.
  • Redirect URL matches a sketchy non-governmental site (`post-office-redirection-fees.info`).
  • Urgent payment required to release delivery.
